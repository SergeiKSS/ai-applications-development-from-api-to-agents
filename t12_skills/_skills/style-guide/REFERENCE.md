# Style Guide Reference

## Brand Voice at a Glance

| Attribute      | Do                                                    | Don't                                           |
|-----------------|--------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------|
| Voice           | Sound like a knowledgeable, friendly colleague          | Sound like a legal disclaimer or a press release  |
| Tone            | Warm, direct, confident                                | Overly formal, apologetic, or hyped-up           |
| Sentences       | Short, one idea per sentence                            | Long compound sentences stuffed with clauses      |
| Vocabulary      | Plain English, everyday words                           | Jargon, buzzwords, internal acronyms              |
| Point of view   | "You" for the reader, "we" for the company              | Third person ("the user", "the company")          |
| Contractions    | Use them (`we're`, `you'll`, `don't`)                    | Spell everything out ("we are", "you will")       |

## Content Types

### Emails

- **Subject lines:** Keep to 6–8 words / under 50 characters. Avoid ALL CAPS, excessive punctuation
  (`!!!`), and clickbait ("You won't believe...").
- **Body:**
  - Structure: one-sentence context → the point/update → a single clear call to action (CTA).
  - CTA format: a short imperative sentence or button-style phrase ("Reset your password",
    "See your invoice") — never vague ("Click here").
  - Sign-off: short and human ("Thanks," / "Talk soon,") followed by a first name, not "The Team".
- **Tone dial:**
  - *Transactional* (receipts, confirmations): neutral, factual, minimal personality.
  - *Marketing*: upbeat but not hyped; lead with the reader's benefit, not the feature.
  - *Support*: empathy-first — acknowledge the issue before explaining the fix.

### Blog Posts

- **Structure:** One H1 (the title), H2s for main sections, H3s only for sub-points. Intro is 2–3
  sentences that state what the reader will learn — no throat-clearing.
- **Style:** Write at an 8th–10th grade reading level. Spell out acronyms on first use, then use
  the short form.
- **Headings:** Sentence case (not Title Case). Make headings outcome-focused — describe what the
  reader gets, not the topic label (e.g. "Cut deploy time in half" instead of "Deployment
  Optimization").

### Social Media

- **LinkedIn:** Aim for 1,300 characters or fewer displayed before "see more". Open with a hook
  line (a question or bold claim), not a greeting. Use 3–5 relevant hashtags at the end, never
  mid-sentence. Emoji sparingly, as bullet markers or emphasis only.
- **Twitter/X:** Keep to a single clear idea per post (under 280 characters). Open with the point,
  not a lead-in. Use 1–2 hashtags max. Emoji only if they replace a word naturally.

### Support / Help Content

- **Tone:** Empathy-first, then solution-first — acknowledge the frustration in one sentence, then
  move immediately to the fix.
- **Language:** Use partnering phrases ("Let's fix this together", "Here's what we'll do") instead
  of blame-shifting or passive phrasing ("It appears the setting was misconfigured").
- **Format:** Numbered steps for any multi-step fix. **Bold** UI elements and button labels exactly
  as they appear on screen. Close with an offer for further help ("Still stuck? Reply here and
  we'll jump in.").

## Grammar & Mechanics

- **Punctuation:** Use the Oxford comma. Use an em dash (—) for an aside instead of parentheses
  when it reads more naturally. Avoid exclamation marks except in casual social copy, and never
  more than one in a row.
- **Capitalization:** Sentence case for headings and subject lines. Capitalize product/feature
  names exactly as branded; don't capitalize generic nouns around them.
- **Numbers:** Spell out one through nine; use digits for 10 and above. Always use digits for
  stats, versions, dates, and measurements regardless of value ("3 steps", "v2", "5 GB").
- **Inclusive language:** Default to "they/them" when a person's pronouns are unknown. Prefer
  neutral role terms ("teammate", "customer") over gendered or ability-based idioms.
